# P1: Design and statistics of performance measurement

## Method caveat

Eight papers were downloaded and converted to text locally, so their findings come from the paper text: S1 (Georges et al.), S2 (Mytkowicz et al.), S3 (Kalibera and Jones), S4 (Hoefler and Belli), S5 (Barrett et al.), S6 (Curtsinger and Berger; read for its abstract and statistical argument only), S7 (Maricq et al.), S8 (Daly et al.). The fetch tool returns a model-written extract, not full text; items from it (S9, S10) are marked "extract". Search summaries (S12 to S16) are secondary. S2 came from a course-hosted copy after the publisher page gave only an abstract. The 1986 article on summarizing benchmark results (S11) is paywalled; I used its abstract and the discussion of it in S4. No profiler documentation was read beyond the kernel performance-event page (S9), used as an example of counting versus sampling. Product names appear only as evidence of a hazard. Scaling and the choice of method belong to note p2 and are not repeated. Figures describe hardware and software of 2007 to 2020: they show kinds of effect, not current magnitudes.

## Answer in brief

1. A performance measurement is a sample from a distribution. A claim rests on repeated measurements and states their number, the central value, the dispersion, and how far the central value could be wrong. One run, or the best of several, supports no comparison. [S1, S3, S4, S7]
2. Repeat at each level where variation arises: operation or iteration; process invocation; build (compilation, linking, layout); machine or allocation. Variation between invocations and builds is often larger than between iterations, and repeating only the cheapest level gives an interval that is too narrow. Spend repetition where the variance is, found by a pilot. [S1, S3, S7]
3. Treat early iterations as possibly unrepresentative and fix, before the result is seen, how steady state is judged (a bound on relative dispersion over a window, or changepoint analysis). A run that never meets the criterion is reported as such, not dropped. Do not assume that steady state is reached, or that the same one is reached on every invocation. [S1, S3, S4, S5]
4. State whether a measurement is cold, warm, or both. First-use costs (initialization, compilation at load, cache and page population) are real for some uses and noise for others; report them separately. [S1, S4, S5; corpus: STND.OFFLD.31]
5. The environment biases a measurement independently of the code. The size of an unused environment variable, and the link order of object files, changed run time by tens of percent (and once by almost 300 percent) on several processors, compilers, and a simulator, and flipped conclusions. Compare over several setups, or randomize the setup, and state the claim over that distribution. [S2, S6]
6. A single binary is one sample of the layout space; replicating runs of it does not sample that space. For a small difference, vary layout (link order, randomized placement, rebuilds) or state that it was not varied. [S2, S6]
7. Control what can be controlled and record the rest: clock frequency policy and boost, thermal state, power limits, other load and shared resources, background services, thread and memory placement. A requested frequency is not the frequency the processor runs at; hardware may decide. [S4, S7, S10; corpus: HW.22]
8. Run order matters. In one study, the order of benchmarks changed later results by a factor of three until a reboot. Randomize or interleave the runs of the versions compared, so drift and carry-over fall on both. [S7]
9. The timer is part of the experiment. Its resolution, cost per read, and behavior (monotonic, per-core, synchronized across processes) are known for the target, and the interval is long compared with both. One published guideline: timer cost under 5 percent of the interval, resolution ten times finer. Where an operation is too short, time k executions together, and say that the single-event distribution was lost. [S4, S7]
10. Instrumentation and sampling perturb what they measure. Probes add work; a sampling profiler that samples only at restricted points, or whose attributed instruction lags the event, shows a distorted profile. A profile is checked against a second method or an intervention, and the time of a run is taken with instrumentation off. [S9, S12, S13]
11. Hardware counters are finite. When more events are requested than counters exist, the system time-shares them and each count is an estimate scaled by the fraction of time it ran. The record states which events, whether scaling happened, and what the counter included. [S9]
12. Choose the central value for the question: the arithmetic mean for costs, the harmonic mean for rates, no averaging of ratios (the geometric mean only if only ratios exist, and said so). Report the median and quantiles when the distribution is skewed, multimodal, or the question is about tails. An interval around a mean assumes normality, which timing data often lack even at tens of samples. [S4, S7, S11 abstract]
13. Report dispersion in the units of the quantity, and relative to the center when scales differ. A confidence interval is not the spread of the data. Prefer methods that assume no distribution when the shape is unknown (nonparametric intervals for the median, resampling to size repetitions); check normality before a mean-based interval. [S1, S4, S7]
14. Compare two versions by the difference or ratio with an interval, not by two intervals read by eye and not by a verdict alone. Fix beforehand the size of change that matters, because a large sample makes a trivial difference significant. Control the false-alarm rate across a suite of comparisons. [S1, S3, S4; corpus: STND.VVUQ.35]
15. A benchmark represents something, and the claim names what. A micro-benchmark measures a kernel with warm caches and predictable branches; an application benchmark measures a mix; neither stands for the other. Benchmarks, inputs, and sizes are chosen and justified before results are seen, and a reported subset gives the reason. [S4, S1]
16. A micro-benchmark can measure nothing: the compiler may delete unused work, hoist invariant work out of the timed loop, or fold work on constants. Check the benchmark (generated code; time that scales with size; inputs unknown at translation time). Library helpers that force a result into memory do not stop optimization of the expression, so constants can still be folded. The output of the timed code is also checked for correctness. [S5, S9 extract, S14 secondary]
17. Record enough to repeat the measurement: repetitions at each level and their nesting; the steady-state criterion and where it cut; timer, resolution, and overhead; factors controlled and not, and their levels; run order; layout variation; statistics used; and the raw samples. Naming a well-known system without its software state is insufficient. Keep the raw samples, since a different summary or a later question needs them. [S4, S3, S5, S8]
18. Detect a regression against a recorded history, not one previous run or a fixed threshold. Noise differs between benchmarks and over time, so a fixed threshold either misses small changes or raises false alarms; changepoint analysis, with no distributional assumption beyond a finite mean, found smaller regressions with far fewer false alarms in a large industrial series. Noise has platform, software, and load-generator parts that can be reduced, not removed. Attribute a regression to a revision by repeating measurement at the revisions between the last good and first bad point. [S8]
19. Rerunning a failed performance check until it passes is selection. A rerun rule recorded beforehand, with its false-alarm and detection rates, is evidence; an unrecorded one is not. [S3, S8; corpus: VV.3, NUMER.26]
20. A change of machine, firmware, kernel, compiler, or library is a change to the experiment. The series is restarted, or the change is marked as a changepoint. [S7, S8]

## Timeless versus moving

Stable: a measurement is a sample and needs repetition and a statement of spread; variation arises at several levels; the setup can bias a result and the remedy is to vary it and say what was not varied; timer resolution and overhead bound what can be measured; the mean must match the unit and ratios are not averaged; fixed thresholds do not suit series with unequal, changing noise; a compiler may remove or fold work with an unused or constant result.

Moving: how systems warm up (run-time compilation, adaptive hardware); frequency and power management, which hardware decides more of each generation; counter hardware and sampling support; layout-randomization tools and profilers; statistical practice, which moved from normal-theory intervals toward nonparametric and resampling methods; and shared cloud allocations as the usual environment.

Consequence for a standard: state what must be shown (levels and counts of repetition, the steady-state criterion, factors varied or fixed, adequacy of the timer, the statistic and its interval, the practical threshold, raw samples, the history for regressions), name no test, library, counter interface, or tool, and give no threshold as a requirement. Figures such as 5 percent timer overhead, 30 runs, or a coefficient of variation of 0.02 are examples in the literature, not norms.
